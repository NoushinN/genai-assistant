# Appendix B: Glossary of Key Terms {-}

This glossary defines terms as they are used throughout this book. Definitions are intentionally practical, governance-oriented, and context-specific rather than technical or vendor-dependent.

The glossary reflects the central perspective developed throughout the book:

> Generative AI systems may assist analytical work, but responsibility for evidence, interpretation, reasoning, and accountability remains human.

---

## AI and analytical workflow

### Assistant

A role assigned to a generative AI system in this book. An assistant supports inquiry, drafting, organization, and reflection but does not determine analytical direction, validate conclusions, or assume responsibility for outcomes.

---

### Generative AI system

A software system that produces text or other outputs by generating statistically likely continuations based on patterns learned from data. In this book, generative AI systems are treated as tools for supporting analytical work rather than sources of understanding, expertise, or authority.

---

### Prompt (as a research instrument)

A structured input used to guide interaction with a generative AI system. Like other research instruments, prompts frame tasks, constrain outputs, encode assumptions, and shape what becomes analytically visible. Prompt design influences inquiry but does not confer validity on generated outputs.

---

### AI-assisted analysis

Analytical work in which generative AI contributes to activities such as:
- drafting,
- synthesis,
- organization,
- comparison,
- or exploratory inquiry

while responsibility for evidence, interpretation, and conclusions remains human.

---

### Workflow stage

A distinct phase within the AI-assisted analytical lifecycle. In this book, workflow stages typically include:
- drafting and framing,
- verification,
- reasoning and uncertainty review,
- and final sign-off or disclosure.

---

### Human sign-off

The explicit assignment of responsibility and approval to a human analyst, reviewer, or institution before AI-assisted analytical work is circulated, relied upon, or published.

---

## Governance and accountability

### Governance boundary

The conceptual and operational limit defining how generative AI may appropriately participate in analytical work. In this book, governance boundaries distinguish between:
- acceptable assistance,
- conditional or high-review uses,
- and non-delegable human responsibilities

such as validation, authoritative interpretation, and decision-making.

---

### Conditional or high-review use

AI-assisted analytical activity that may be appropriate only with additional safeguards such as:
- evidence traceability,
- uncertainty disclosure,
- peer review,
- documentation,
- or formal sign-off

because of its interpretive or institutional impact.

---

### Accountability

The assignment of responsibility for:
- analytical framing,
- interpretation,
- reasoning,
- evidence use,
- uncertainty communication,
- and conclusions.

In AI-assisted work, accountability always remains human regardless of the extent of AI involvement.

---

### Analytical integrity

The preservation of:
- evidentiary discipline,
- methodological rigor,
- transparency,
- uncertainty awareness,
- and accountable reasoning

throughout analytical workflows.

---

### Disclosure

The practice of communicating how generative AI assistance was used within an analytical workflow. Disclosure should be proportional to the role AI played and should support transparency, reviewability, and accountability.

---

### Review discipline

A structured process of verifying, questioning, revising, and evaluating AI-assisted outputs before they are accepted into analytical work. Review discipline includes:
- evidence checking,
- uncertainty assessment,
- reasoning evaluation,
- governance review,
- and accountability review.

---

### Governance escalation

The principle that review intensity, documentation requirements, and accountability expectations should increase as analytical work moves closer to:
- interpretation,
- recommendations,
- operational influence,
- or decision-making authority.

---

## Evidence and reasoning

### Evidence vs. interpretation

A distinction between:
- source material (data, documents, empirical findings), and
- the analytical judgments drawn from them.

Generative AI can assist with summarizing or reorganizing evidence, but interpretation and justification remain human responsibilities.

---

### Evidence traceability

The ability to connect analytical claims, summaries, interpretations, or recommendations back to identifiable and reviewable evidence sources.

---

### Traceability

The ability to link analytical claims back to their:
- sources,
- assumptions,
- reasoning processes,
- or explicit judgments.

Maintaining traceability helps ensure that AI-assisted outputs remain interpretable, reviewable, and accountable.

---

### Traceability ladder

A conceptual model showing how analytical work moves from:
- primary evidence,
- toward synthesis,
- interpretation,
- and decision-support language.

As analytical distance from source material increases, uncertainty and review requirements also increase.

---

### Verification

The process of checking factual claims against:
- primary sources,
- authoritative references,
- or validated evidence.

Verification is distinct from validation: generative AI outputs require human verification and cannot validate themselves.

---

### Validation

The process of confirming that analytical conclusions, methods, or outputs are appropriate, defensible, and institutionally acceptable. In this book, validation remains a human responsibility and cannot be delegated to generative AI systems.

---

### Uncertainty visibility

The practice of making:
- limitations,
- ambiguity,
- assumptions,
- evidence gaps,
- and unresolved questions

explicit within analytical work rather than smoothing them away through confident language.

---

### Epistemic humility

An orientation toward knowledge that treats conclusions as:
- provisional,
- evidence-dependent,
- revisable,
- and open to scrutiny.

Epistemic humility underpins responsible AI-assisted analytical practice throughout this book.

---

## Failure modes and safeguards

### Failure mode

A recurring pattern by which AI-assisted analysis can go wrong, often subtly. Failure modes discussed in this book include:
- fluency masking uncertainty,
- framing lock-in,
- false balance,
- invisible assumptions,
- automation complacency,
- and diffusion of responsibility.

---

### Fluency masking uncertainty

A failure mode in which coherent and confident AI-generated prose obscures:
- evidentiary weakness,
- ambiguity,
- incomplete reasoning,
- or unresolved assumptions.

---

### Framing lock-in

A failure mode in which early AI-generated framing choices narrow subsequent analysis by shaping:
- which questions appear important,
- which interpretations remain visible,
- or which alternatives are considered legitimate.

---

### False balance

A failure mode in which competing positions are presented as equally credible despite substantial differences in:
- evidence quality,
- methodological rigor,
- or empirical support.

---

### Invisible assumptions

Assumptions embedded within prompts, institutional norms, training data, or analytical conventions that remain unstated yet still shape interpretation and conclusions.

---

### Diffusion of responsibility

A failure mode in which ownership of analytical choices becomes blurred because AI-generated outputs are implicitly treated as responsible for wording, framing, or conclusions.

---

### Automation complacency

Gradual reduction in review intensity or analytical skepticism caused by repeated exposure to fluent and plausible AI-generated outputs.

---

### Mitigation practice

A review or governance strategy intended to reduce the likelihood or impact of analytical failure modes. Mitigation practices discussed in this book include:
- evidence verification,
- explicit uncertainty review,
- assumption checks,
- peer review,
- and accountable sign-off.

---

## Core governing principle

### “Generative AI assists; analysts decide.”

The central governing principle of this book. Generative AI systems may support:
- drafting,
- organization,
- synthesis,
- comparison,
- and exploratory inquiry,

but responsibility for:
- evidence,
- interpretation,
- reasoning,
- conclusions,
- and analytical impact

remains human.
